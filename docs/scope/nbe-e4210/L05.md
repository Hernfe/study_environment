## Checklist

Legend: S = slide, T = textbook book pages read, L = lectured (slides 1
to 20), A = TA addendum (slides 22 to 39), HW = HW3 Exercise 1.

### 1. What sound is (S 4; L)

- [x] Sound as compression and rarefaction of air; frequency (Hz) is pitch; amplitude is intensity (dB, loudness); phase. S4. T pp. 370-372.
- [x] Audible range 20 Hz to 20 kHz; intensity range about a trillion to one. T p. 372. One line each.
- Box 11.1 (ultrasound, infrasound) out: boxes excluded.

### 2. Ear anatomy and the path of sound (S 6, S 28; L, S28 A)

- [x] Outer ear (pinna, auditory canal), middle ear (tympanic membrane, ossicles), inner ear (cochlea, medial to the oval window). S6. T pp. 373-374. HW Figure 1 names these seven structures.
- [x] What each part does: outer ear collects, tympanic membrane vibrates, ossicles transmit and amplify force, stapes drives the oval window, cochlear fluid moves the basilar membrane. S6. T p. 373 (the five-step chain).
- [x] Air to bone to fluid to membrane to hair cell. S28 (A).
- [x] The auditory-vestibular nerve (cranial nerve VIII) leaves the cochlea. S6 label. T pp. 373, 382.
- [x] Auditory and visual pathways compared: receptors, early integration, thalamic relay (MGN versus LGN), cortex. T p. 374. One line.

### 3. Middle ear and impedance matching (S 7, S 9; L)

- [x] Malleus, incus, stapes; footplate of the stapes at the oval window. T p. 374.
- [x] Acoustic impedance: air < fluid < solid; sound passing into a higher impedance loses energy; air-borne sound would be mostly reflected at the fluid-filled cochlea. S7. T p. 375 (about 0.1 % of the energy would enter).
- [x] Ossicles amplify pressure: lever action raises force, the small footplate concentrates it; pressure at the oval window about 20 times that at the tympanic membrane. S7 ("How?"). T p. 375.
- [x] Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the nasal cavity and equalises pressure (yawning, swallowing). S9 (title and 3D link only). T p. 374. One detail block.

### 4. The acoustic reflex (S 8; L)

- [x] Tensor tympani (on the malleus) and stapedius (on the stapes) stiffen the chain; attenuation greater at low frequencies. S8. T p. 376.
- [x] Functions: prevent damage; adapt to low-frequency noise (speech in noise). Caveats: muscles tire; latency 50 to 100 ms, so sudden sounds still damage. S8. T pp. 376-377.
- [x] The textbook's name is attenuation reflex; the slide's is acoustic reflex. Both given.

### 5. Cochlea, scalae and fluids (S 10 to 13; L)

- [x] Cochlea: coiled tube, about 32 mm, base with oval and round windows, apex with the helicotrema. S10. T pp. 377-378.
- [x] Scala vestibuli, scala media, scala tympani; Reissner's membrane and basilar membrane; organ of Corti on the basilar membrane, tectorial membrane above; stria vascularis. S12, S13. T p. 377.
- [x] Perilymph (scala vestibuli and tympani): Na+ rich, K+ poor, like CSF (K+ 7 mM, Na+ 140 mM). Endolymph (scala media): K+ rich (150 mM), Na+ poor (1 mM), made by the stria vascularis; about 80 mV positive to perilymph (endocochlear potential). S13, S18, S19. T p. 378.
- [x] Round window: incompressible fluid, so an inward stapes bulges the round window out. S11 (AI picture not used; its text "is correct" per the slide). T p. 379.

### 6. The cochlea as a frequency analyzer; tonotopy (S 10, S 14, S 16; L)

- [x] Basilar membrane wider at the apex (about 5 times), stiffer at the base (about 100 times): base stiff and narrow, high frequencies; apex floppy and wide, low frequencies. S14. T p. 379.
- [x] Travelling wave from base toward apex; high frequencies dissipate near the base, low ones travel to the apex; place code. S16 (Figure 11.9). T pp. 379-380.
- [x] Tonotopy defined; maps on the basilar membrane, in each relay nucleus, the MGN and auditory cortex. S14 (textbook wording). T p. 380.

### 7. Organ of Corti (S 17, S 18; L)

- [x] Hair cells, rods of Corti, supporting cells; reticular lamina; stereocilia; inner hair cells one row (about 3500 on S18, 4500 in the book), outer hair cells three rows (about 12,000). S17, S18. T pp. 380-382.
- [x] Inner hair cells the main sensory output; outer hair cells an active mechanical amplifier; stereocilia bend relative to the tectorial membrane; hair cells do not fire action potentials (not neurons). S17. T p. 380.
- [x] Spiral ganglion in the modiolus; bipolar; axons form the auditory nerve. T p. 382.

### 8. Hair cell transduction and the K+ story (S 19; L)

- [x] Deflection toward the tallest stereocilia stretches tip links, mechanically gated (MET) channels open, K+ enters from endolymph, depolarization, voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open, glutamate excites spiral ganglion afferents. S19. T pp. 384-385.
- [x] Why K+ depolarizes: endolymph K+ rich and about 80 mV positive; the cell inside about 125 mV negative relative to endolymph; K+ movement fast and passive; leaves at the base into K+-poor perilymph, so no pump or ATP is needed to recycle it. S19. T pp. 385-386 (EK about 0 mV at the apical membrane).
- [x] Receptor potential graded, saturates at about 20 nm; threshold about 0.3 nm. T p. 384. One detail line.
- [x] Receptor potential follows the sound waveform at low frequency (Figure 11.14b); hair cell resting potential about -70 mV. T p. 384.

### 9. Compression, rarefaction and shear (S 20, S 22; L, S22 A)

- [x] Slide 20's convention, stated verbatim: compression moves the basilar membrane down, shear pushes the tip links, MET channels close, hair cell hyperpolarizes. Rarefaction moves the basilar membrane up, shear pulls the tip links, channels open, hair cell depolarizes. S20. T pp. 382-384 (Figure 11.13: basilar membrane up, stereocilia bend outward, depolarization).
- [x] Shear, not approach: the basilar and tectorial membranes move differently; their relative sliding bends the stereocilia. S22 (A). T p. 384.
- [x] Outer hair cell tips in the tectorial membrane; inner hair cell tips bent by moving endolymph. T p. 384. One detail line.

### 10. Outer hair cells and prestin (S 23 to 26; A)

- [x] OHCs about 3 times more numerous than IHCs but not the primary encoders; efferent input from the brain (medial olivocochlear system; acetylcholine). S23, S24. T pp. 386, 388.
- [x] Prestin electromotility: depolarization shortens, hyperpolarization lengthens; driven by the receptor potential, no ATP, fast. S23 to S25. T p. 386.
- [x] Both shortening and lengthening add energy when timed (swing analogy); amplification is not the membranes moving closer. S25 (A).
- [x] Cochlear amplifier: without it about 100-fold smaller basilar membrane motion; prestin knockout mice nearly deaf; antibiotics (kanamycin) damage OHCs; furosemide reduces transduction and basilar membrane motion. T pp. 386-388.
- [x] Efferents modulate rather than drive each contraction. S26 (A).
- [x] OHCs increase sensitivity and sharpen frequency tuning. S23, S27 (A).
- Box 11.3 (otoacoustic emissions) out; S18's label "otoacoustic emissions" is kept as one line, not quizzed.

### 11. Inner versus outer hair cells (S 27; A)

- [x] IHC: about 95 % of auditory nerve afferents; convert basilar membrane motion into neural output; damage gives major hearing loss; one IHC to several (about 10) spiral ganglion cells. OHC: strong efferent input; electromotility; sensitivity and tuning; several OHCs to one spiral ganglion neuron. S27. T p. 386.

### 12. Cochlear implant (S 29; A)

- [x] What if hair cells fail but the auditory nerve works: microphone, processor splitting sound into frequency bands, electrode array stimulating different cochlear locations; exploits tonotopy rather than recreating hair cell mechanics; a neural interface, not a loudspeaker. S29 (A).
- Box 11.2 (cochlear implants) is excluded as a box. Only slide content is used: no electrode counts, no critical period claims, no outcome numbers.

### 13. Phase locking (S 31; A)

- [x] Spikes at a consistent phase of the waveform; receptor potential follows the sound; the slide says it only works up to 3 kHz. S31. T pp. 392-393 (the book gives about 5 kHz).
- [x] A neuron need not fire on every cycle. T p. 393 (Figure 11.21b).

### 14. Spiral ganglion neurons (S 32; A)

- [x] Type I, about 90 to 95 %: myelinated, bipolar, contact one IHC each, each IHC to several type I; carry intensity, frequency, timing. Type II, about 5 to 10 %: thin, unmyelinated, contact many OHCs; respond to intense sounds, may signal damage; role under investigation. S32. T p. 386 (the book's 95 % and 5 %).

### 15. Labelled-line coding and characteristic frequency (S 33, S 34; A)

- [x] Labelled line: each fibre comes from one place, so its activity signals one frequency band; tonotopy kept in the auditory nerve and the cochlear nucleus (Figure 11.20, anterior low to posterior high CF). S33. T pp. 391-392.
- [x] Characteristic frequency: the frequency needing the least intensity (slide's definition); tuning curve; louder sound raises the rate and widens the response. S34. T pp. 390-391.
- [x] Tonotopy preserved cochlea to brainstem to MGN to A1. S34.

### 16. Central auditory pathway (S 35; A, HW reading)

- [x] Spiral ganglion, cochlear nuclei (dorsal and ventral, ipsilateral, medulla), superior olive (first binaural), lateral lemniscus, inferior colliculus (all routes converge), MGN, A1. S35. T p. 389.
- [x] Bilateral early; a unilateral central lesion rarely causes deafness in one ear; only cochlear nucleus or auditory nerve damage does. S35. T p. 389.
- [x] Feedback: brain stem to OHCs, cortex to MGN and inferior colliculus. T p. 389. One line.
- Binaural mechanisms of the superior olive for localization (pp. 393-399) out: sound localization excluded.

### 17. Loudness (S 36; A)

- [x] Rate code (larger receptor potential, higher rate until saturation) and population code (wider basilar membrane motion recruits more fibres); no single volume neuron. S36. T p. 391.
- [x] Attenuation reflex and dynamic range link back to section 4. T p. 376.

### 18. Two frequency codes; the volley principle (S 37; A)

- [x] Place (tonotopy): where; essential across the spectrum, especially high frequencies. Timing (phase locking): when; mainly low frequencies; populations volley across cycles. S37. T pp. 391-393.
- [x] Volley principle: several phase-locked neurons, each firing on some cycles, together mark every cycle. S37 ("volley"), T p. 393 (named).
- [x] Why tonotopy alone is not enough: no CF below about 200 Hz; peak place shifts with intensity. T p. 392.
- [x] Low frequencies phase locking; intermediate both; high tonotopy alone. T p. 393.

### 19. Auditory cortex (S 38; A, HW reading)

- [x] A1 in the superior temporal lobe (Brodmann 41); MGN projects via the acoustic radiation (internal capsule); layer IV receives MGN input. S38. T p. 399.
- [x] Tonotopy in A1: isofrequency bands; low frequencies rostral and lateral, high caudal and medial; columns of similar CF. S38. T p. 400.
- [x] Cortical neurons respond to complex combinations (frequency, timing, intensity tuning, clicks, vocalizations); secondary areas more abstract; Wernicke's area interprets speech. S38. T pp. 400-402.
- [x] Binaural columns mentioned only as an organising principle; their localization role out.
- Box 11.5 (bats) out.

### 20. What cortical damage reveals (S 39; A, HW reading)

- [x] Bilateral A1 ablation: deafness (slide: severe impairment of conscious auditory perception). Unilateral: little loss of detection, localization impaired, frequency and intensity discrimination near normal; contrast with V1 (hemifield blindness). S39. T pp. 402-403.
- [x] Hearing versus understanding; central disorders with an intact cochlea; speech and music networks. S39.
- The localization deficit is named as a consequence only, since the slide names it; its mechanism is not taught. Box 11.6 (deafness types, tinnitus) out.

## Scope doubts for the student to check

1. Compression versus rarefaction (S20) reverses the usual textbook picture of which phase opens the channels. Bear never ties a pressure phase to a direction; it only states basilar membrane up, depolarization (Figure 11.13). The page and the quiz use the slide's mapping (compression, down, close, hyperpolarize; rarefaction, up, open, depolarize), because the slide is lectured content. Other sources tie compression to downward motion too, but the exam could phrase this differently.
2. Phase locking limit: the slide says up to 3 kHz, the book about 5 kHz. The page gives 3 kHz as the course figure and names 5 kHz as the book's; questions use "about 3 kHz" and never hinge on the difference.
3. Inner hair cell count: S18 says about 3500, the book about 4500. Numbers are not quizzed.
4. Spiral ganglion types: S32 gives type I about 90 to 95 % and type II about 5 to 10 %; the book says more than 95 % and less than 5 %. The quiz uses "most" and "a small minority".
5. Characteristic frequency: S34 defines it as the frequency needing the least intensity; Bear (p. 390) as the frequency of greatest response. The page gives the slide's definition and notes the book's.
6. Slides 22 to 39 were never lectured. They are in scope by the TA's statement, but the exam may weigh lectured material (pages 370-386) more. Hard questions draw on both.
7. The cochlear implant appears on S29 but Box 11.2 is excluded. Only the slide's four points and its "not a loudspeaker" line are used; the clinical case asks what the device needs (an intact nerve, tonotopy) and what it cannot restore (hair cell mechanics, the cochlear amplifier's fine tuning). The last point is inference from S27 and S29 together.
8. S11 is an AI picture of the round window; the slide says the text is correct but the cochlea is not. Only the text is used, backed by T p. 379.
