### 2. Synapse anatomy and chemical transmission (N 3.1, S 7-9, 14-15)

- [x] Synapse: junction where a neuron communicates with a neuron, muscle or gland cell; presynaptic sends, postsynaptic receives; one-way flow. S7 (Figure 2.7, axon in contact with dendrite, not continuity). N 3.1. T p. 111.
- [x] Axodendritic (stereotypical), axosomatic, axoaxonic, dendrodendritic synapses. S8-9. N 3.1, 3.3. T pp. 115-117.
- [x] Chemical synapse components (Figure 5.4): axon terminal (presynaptic element), synaptic vesicles (about 50 nm), secretory granules (about 100 nm, dense core, peptides), mitochondria, synaptic cleft (20-50 nm), active zone, postsynaptic density with receptors, membrane differentiations, postsynaptic dendrite. S14. N 3.1. T pp. 114-116.
- [x] Electron micrograph: presynaptic terminal with vesicles and mitochondria, active zone, postsynaptic cell. S15. N 3.1. T p. 116.
- [x] Release: AP depolarizes the terminal, voltage-gated Ca2+ channels at the active zones open, Ca2+ triggers exocytosis. S14 text and S24. N 3.1. T p. 122.
- [x] Receptors: transmitter-gated ion channels (direct) versus G-protein-coupled receptors (indirect); the receptor decides the response. N 3.1. T pp. 115, 124, 127.

### 3. Electrical synapses and gap junctions (N 3.2, S 10-13)

- [x] Gap junction: 6 connexins make a connexon, 2 connexons (one per cell) make a gap junction channel, many channels make a gap junction; 3.5 nm gap, 20 nm channel, pore 1-2 nm; ions and small molecules pass; bidirectional (Figure 5.1). S10. N 3.2. T pp. 111-112.
- [x] Electrical PSP: an AP in cell 1 drives current through the junction into cell 2, about 1 mV (from -65 to -64 mV) with a small undershoot; "mind the y scale"; pre = source, post = target; usually too small to fire alone but many sum. S11 (Figure 5.2). N 3.2. T pp. 112-113.
- [x] Function: synchronization of oscillations and spikes, inferior olive; without gap junctions (Cx36 deletion) cells still oscillate and fire but uncoordinated (Figure 5.3); spike synchrony and phase synchrony. S12-13. N 3.2. T pp. 113-114.
- [x] Electrical synapses fast, nearly fail-safe if large; common in development; interneuron coupling (S48). T p. 112-113. N 7.3.

### 4. Synaptic targeting and convergence (N 3.3, S 16-17)

- [x] Figure 5.6: axodendritic (a), axosomatic (b), axoaxonic (c). S16. T p. 116.
- [x] Figure 5.7: (a) one terminal on a dendritic spine (axospinous), (b) one axon with two terminals on one soma, (c) a large terminal surrounding a soma, (d) one terminal contacting several spines; larger synapses have more active zones. S17. N 3.3. T pp. 116-117.
- [x] Convergence: thousands of inputs on one neuron; synaptic integration turns them into one output. N 3.3. T p. 132.
- [x] Effect of a synapse depends on distance from the spike-initiation zone and dendritic properties. N 3.3. T pp. 133-134.
- [x] Asymmetric (Gray's type I, usually excitatory) versus symmetric (type II, usually inhibitory) synapses: structural clues, not definitions. N 3.3. T pp. 117-119, 138. FLAG: notes only; one compare row.

### 5. Dendritic spines (N 3.4, S 18-20)

- [x] Spines: small projections receiving synaptic input; head on a narrow neck; Cajal's drawings and a Golgi-stained dendrite (Yuste 2015). S18. N 3.4. T pp. 46, 118.
- [x] Spine ultrastructure (rapid Golgi and EM, Harris): axonal bouton, dendritic spine, PSD, vesicles in the axon, spine apparatus, mitochondrion in the dendrite, glia; 1 micron scale. S19. N 3.4. T pp. 117-118.
- [x] Spines may isolate chemical reactions near the synapse; their structure changes with activity. N 3.4. T p. 46.
- [x] 3D electron microscopy: serial sections reconstruct a dendrite with synapses (red) and organelles; shows contacts, not the size of the electrical response. S20. N 3.4. T pp. 118, 132-133.

### 6. Transmitter identification, synthesis and transport (N 4.1, S 21-23)

- [x] Three criteria for a transmitter: synthesized and stored presynaptically, released on stimulation, applied molecule mimics the response. N 4.1. T p. 145.
- [x] Table 5.1: amino acids (GABA, glutamate, glycine), amines (ACh, dopamine, epinephrine, histamine, norepinephrine, serotonin), peptides (CCK, dynorphin, enkephalins, NAAG, neuropeptide Y, somatostatin, substance P, TRH, VIP). ACh is grouped with amines but is not an amine. S21 (Figure 5.10 structures). N 4.1. T pp. 120-121.
- [x] Synthesis and storage (Figure 5.11): peptides made as precursors on the rough ER, split in the Golgi, packed in secretory granules, transported to the terminal; amines and amino acids made by enzymes in the terminal cytosol and loaded into vesicles by transporter proteins. S22. N 4.1. T pp. 122-123.
- [x] Anterograde transport of loaded vesicles by kinesin along microtubules (Figure 2.18); retrograde back to the soma. S23. N 4.1. T pp. 43-44.
- [x] The transmitter family does not fix the effect; the receptor does (ACh slows the heart via a G-protein-coupled K+ channel, excites skeletal muscle via a Na+-permeable channel). N 4.1. T p. 128.

### 7. Calcium-dependent release and vesicle fusion (N 4.2, S 24-25)

- [x] Figure 5.12: (1) loaded vesicle, (2) Ca2+ influx through voltage-gated calcium channels, (3) exocytosis at the active zone, (4) recycling by endocytosis. S24. N 4.2. T pp. 122-124.
- [x] Speed: exocytosis within 0.2 ms of Ca2+ influx in squid, about 60 us in mammals at body temperature; Ca2+ microdomain above 0.01 mM at the active zone; docked vesicles; reserve pool on the cytoskeleton. N 4.2. T pp. 124-125.
- [x] SNAREs (Box 5.3): v-SNAREs on the vesicle, t-SNAREs on the target membrane bind and dock the vesicle; synaptotagmin is the Ca2+ sensor; fusion pore; yeast uses the same machinery, conserved over a billion years. S25. N 4.2. T p. 125.
- [x] Quantum: the contents of one vesicle; minis; NMJ releases about 200 vesicles per AP (EPSP 40 mV), CNS synapses often one (tenths of a mV). N 4.2. T pp. 132-133. FLAG: quantal analysis is notes plus textbook; kept as one definition.
- [x] Peptides released from granules away from active zones, need high-frequency trains, slow (50 ms or more). T p. 124. FLAG: textbook only; one line in detail.
- [x] Botulinum toxin cleaves SNAREs and blocks release (Box 5.5). N 4.2. T p. 131. One line.

### 8. Postsynaptic receptor mechanisms and signal termination (N 4.3, S 26)

- [x] ACh-gated channel: five subunits round a pore (Figure 5.14); transmitter-gated ion channel = ionotropic receptor; pore opens within microseconds. S26. N 4.3. T pp. 124, 126.
- [x] EPSP: transient depolarization toward threshold (Na+-permeable channels; ACh, glutamate). IPSP: transient hyperpolarization (Cl- channels; GABA, glycine). S26. N 4.3. T p. 126.
- [x] G-protein-coupled (metabotropic) receptors: transmitter binds, G-protein activated, effector = G-protein-gated channel or enzyme making second messengers; slower, longer, more diverse (Figure 5.17). S52. N 4.3, 8.2. T pp. 126-127.
- [x] Autoreceptors on the terminal reduce release. T pp. 128-130. FLAG: textbook only; homework arrow on S6 points at "Autoreceptors" and "G-protein-coupled receptors" in the chapter list, so it is assigned; one line.
- [x] Removal: diffusion, reuptake by transporters into the terminal or glia, enzymatic destruction (AChE at the NMJ); desensitization if ACh stays. N 4.3. T p. 130.
- [x] Neuropharmacology: inhibitors, receptor antagonists (curare), agonists (nicotine). N 8.1. T pp. 130-131, 150.

### 9. EPSPs and IPSPs (N 5.1-5.2, S 27-28)

- [x] EPSP generation (Figure 5.15): AP in the terminal, transmitter binds, Na+ enters through transmitter-gated channels, Vm rises from -65 mV and returns; ENa = +62 mV circled. S27. N 5.1. T p. 128.
- [x] IPSP generation (Figure 5.16): Cl- enters through transmitter-gated Cl- channels, Vm falls toward ECl = -65 mV. S28. N 5.2. T p. 129.
- [x] Reversal potential (Box 5.4): the Vm at which the net current through a channel reverses; I-V plot; mixed Na+/K+ channel reverses near 0 mV so current is inward at rest; excitatory if E_rev above threshold, inhibitory if below. N 5.2, 9.2. T p. 127. Learning objective 3.
- [x] Inhibition without hyperpolarization: if Vm = ECl, no IPSP but conductance rises; K+-permeable inhibitory channels pull toward EK. N 5.2. T pp. 127, 136-137.

### 10. Recording transmitter-gated channels (N 5.3, S 29)

- [x] Patch clamp of a transmitter-gated channel (Figure 5.18): baseline closed, downward steps inward current when open, similar amplitudes, variable timing; channels flip between open and closed while transmitter is present. S29. N 5.3. T pp. 94-95, 132.
- [x] Slide also shows the L02 patch figure and titles it "patching the Na+ channel"; transmitter-gated channels need not be Na+-selective (ACh channel passes Na+ and K+). N 5.3. T p. 124.
- [x] Summed current of many channels depolarizes the membrane when Vm is free (EPSP); the clamped trace shows current, not the EPSP. N 5.3. T p. 132.

### 11. Spatial and temporal summation (N 5.4, S 30)

- [x] Figure 5.19: one EPSP; spatial summation of simultaneous EPSPs from several synapses; temporal summation of EPSPs from the same synapse within 1-15 ms. S30. N 5.4. T pp. 133-134.
- [x] Synaptic integration; a summed EPSP fires a spike only if threshold is reached at the spike-initiation zone. N 5.4. T pp. 132-133.

### 12. Dendritic spread and shunting inhibition (N 5.5, S 31-32)

- [x] Passive cable (Figure 5.20): current leaks across the membrane, EPSP shrinks with distance; length constant lambda, depolarization falls to 37 percent at one lambda; Vx = Vo / e^(x/lambda). S31. N 5.5. T pp. 134-135.
- [x] Lambda rises with membrane resistance rm and falls with internal resistance ri; wide dendrites have low ri; rm changes with open channels, so lambda is not constant. N 5.5. T p. 135.
- [x] Active dendrites: voltage-gated Na+, Ca2+, K+ channels amplify distant EPSPs; signals also travel from soma back into dendrites ("effects go both ways"). S31 text. N 5.5. T p. 136.
- [x] Shunting inhibition (Figure 5.21): inhibitory synapse between the excitatory input and the soma; open Cl- channels at ECl near rest lower membrane resistance, Cl- entry opposes the depolarization, the somatic EPSP is suppressed while the dendritic EPSP remains. S32. N 5.5. T pp. 136-138.
- [x] "Potential at the axon hillock is all that counts"; EPSPs and IPSPs compete along the depolarization-hyperpolarization axis; near-soma projecting FS-PV interneurons have a veto right. S32 text. N 5.5. T p. 138.

### 13. Organizing transmitter systems (N 6.1, S 33-36)

- [x] The synapse once more (Figure 6.1 on S34): transmitter-synthesizing enzymes, vesicle transporters, reuptake transporters, degradative enzymes, transmitter-gated channels, G-protein-coupled receptors, G-proteins, G-protein-gated channels, second messenger cascades. S34. N 6.1. T p. 144.
- [x] Glutamate mediates most fast excitation; AMPA, NMDA, kainate receptors named after agonists; glutamate activates all three (Figure 6.x). S35. N 6.1. T pp. 150, 165.
- [x] AMPA channels pass Na+ and K+; at negative Vm more Na+ enters than K+ leaves: net inward current, EPSP. S35, S37. N 6.1. T p. 166.
- [x] GABA mediates most inhibition; GABA-A is a Cl- channel; IPSP when Vm above ECl. S35-36. N 6.1. T pp. 129, 167.
- [x] GABA-A modulatory sites (Figure 6.22): benzodiazepines (more frequent openings), barbiturates (longer openings), ethanol (subunit-dependent), neurosteroids. S36. N 6.1. T pp. 168-169.
- [x] Glycine mediates most of the remaining inhibition. T p. 167. One line.

### 14. Pyramidal neurons and inhibitory circuit motifs (N 6.2, S 37-38)

- [x] Pyramidal neurons excite by releasing glutamate at their terminals; spiny dendrites; long axons to other regions. S37. N 6.2. T p. 48.
- [x] Interneurons inhibit by releasing GABA at their terminals; nine morphological classes on the slide (large basket, small basket, chandelier, neurogliaform, Martinotti, double-bouquet, bipolar, bitufted). S38, S44. N 6.2, 7.2.
- [x] Where inhibition lands matters: basket cells on soma and proximal dendrites, chandelier cells on the axon initial segment, Martinotti cells on dendritic tufts. N 6.2, 7.2. S44 (d). T pp. 137-138.
- [x] Circuit motifs (Tremblay et al.): feedforward inhibition (input excites pyramidal cell and an interneuron that inhibits it), feedback inhibition (pyramidal cells recruit interneurons that inhibit them), disinhibition (an interneuron inhibits another interneuron). N 6.2, 9.5-9.6. S48 (VIP inhibits SOM). FLAG: the motif names are in the notes and self-check, the wiring is on slide 48.
- [x] Excitation-inhibition balance (notes bold line; Box 5.6). N 6.2. T p. 137.

### 15. NMDA receptors and AMPA-NMDA timing (N 6.3, S 39-42)

- [x] NMDA receptor: glutamate-gated cation channel; Mg2+ blocks the pore at negative Vm even with glutamate bound; depolarization (usually from AMPA) expels Mg2+; Na+ and Ca2+ enter, K+ leaves (Figure 6.21, -65 versus -30 mV). S39-41. N 6.3. T pp. 167-168.
- [x] Coincidence detection: glutamate plus depolarization; Ca2+ entry activates enzymes, changes channels and gene expression, basis of lasting change (memory, Chapter 25). N 6.3. T pp. 166-167.
- [x] AMPA and NMDA coexist and both contribute to the EPSP (Figure 6.20); with NMDA blocked (or Mg2+ present alone) the fast EPSP is AMPA. S40-41. N 6.3. T p. 166.
- [x] Time courses (Gerstner Figure 3.2): AMPA fast (ms), NMDA slow and long (tens to hundreds of ms), GABA-A fast, GABA-B slow; NMDA current recorded after cortical stimulation lasts far longer than AMPA. S42. N 9.1.
- [x] GABA-B receptors act through G proteins that open K+ channels (shortcut pathway); slower than GABA-A. N 6.3. T pp. 167, 171-172.
- [x] Excitotoxicity (Box 6.4). T p. 167. FLAG: textbook only; one line in a detail block.

### 16. Interneuron diversity (N 7.1-7.2, S 43-47)

- [x] Interneurons make up 20-30 percent of cortical neurons (Sultan and Shi abstract, highlighted). S43.
- [x] Classification by morphology and connectivity (sometimes laminar location), electrical diversity (active ion-channel combinations plus passive morphology), expression of Ca2+-binding proteins (parvalbumin, calbindin, calretinin), neuropeptides (SST, VIP), receptors (5HT3aR); morphology does not define function. S47. N 7.2. T pp. 46, 48.
- [x] Three groups (Rudy et al.): PV (about 40 percent: basket and chandelier fast-spiking cells), SST (about 30 percent: Martinotti and others), 5HT3aR (about 30 percent: VIP and non-VIP such as neurogliaform). S45. N 7.2.
- [x] Firing patterns: fast spiking (FS), low-threshold spiking (LTS), irregular spiking (IS), bursting (BST), late spiking (LS). S46. N 7.2.
- [x] PV, basket, fast-spiking describe molecular, anatomical and electrical features of overlapping but not identical sets (Petilla terminology). S46-47. N 7.2, 9.3.

### 17. Electrical coupling among interneurons (N 7.3, S 48)

- [x] Wiring: PV inhibits E and PV; SOM inhibits E, PV and VIP; VIP inhibits SOM (disinhibition). S48 left. N 7.3.
- [x] Gap junctions couple interneurons of the same firing class: FS-FS, LTS-LTS, IS-IS, BST-BST, LS-LS. S48 right. N 7.3.

### 18. Receptor pharmacology (N 8.1, S 50-51)

- [x] Agonist mimics, antagonist blocks; drugs distinguish receptor subtypes. N 8.1. T pp. 130-131, 150.
- [x] Table 6.1: ACh nicotinic (nicotine, curare), muscarinic (muscarine, atropine); NE alpha (phenylephrine, phenoxybenzamine), beta (isoproterenol, propranolol); glutamate AMPA (AMPA, CNQX), NMDA (NMDA, AP5); GABA-A (muscimol, bicuculline), GABA-B (baclofen, phaclofen); ATP P2X (ATP, suramin); adenosine A type (adenosine, caffeine). S50. N 8.1. T pp. 150-151.
- [x] Table 6.2: G-protein-coupled receptors: muscarinic M1-M5, mGluR1-8, GABA-B1/B2, 5-HT subtypes, D1-D5, alpha and beta subtypes, opioid, cannabinoid CB1/CB2, P2Y, adenosine A1-A3. S51. T p. 170. Named only, not quizzed beyond the families.
- [x] Adenosine is not packaged in vesicles, so not a classical transmitter. N 8.1. T p. 160. One line.

### 19. G-protein-coupled receptor signalling (N 8.2, S 52)

- [x] Structure: seven membrane-spanning alpha helices, transmitter site outside, G-protein site inside; about 800 genes. T p. 169. FLAG: textbook under S52; one line.
- [x] G-protein cycle: alpha, beta, gamma subunits; GDP at rest; receptor with transmitter swaps GDP for GTP; alpha-GTP and beta-gamma separate and act on effectors; alpha hydrolyses GTP to GDP and rejoins. N 8.2. T pp. 170-171.
- [x] Gs stimulates, Gi inhibits effectors. N 8.3. T p. 170.
- [x] Effectors: shortcut pathway (G-protein-gated ion channel, 30-100 ms, membrane-delimited; muscarinic receptor in the heart, GABA-B) versus second messenger cascades (enzyme such as adenylyl cyclase making cAMP). S52 (Figure 5.17). N 8.2. T pp. 171-172.
- [x] Amplification: one receptor activates 10-20 G-proteins, each cyclase makes many cAMP, each kinase phosphorylates many channels; slower but longer-lasting and regulable. N 8.2. T p. 175.
- [x] Divergence (one transmitter, several receptor subtypes and effectors) and convergence (several transmitters onto one effector). N 8.2. T pp. 176-177.
- [x] Phosphorylation by kinases, reversed by protein phosphatases. N 8.2. T p. 174.

### 20. The norepinephrine beta receptor cascade (N 8.3, S 53)

- [x] Figure 5.22: (1) NE binds the beta receptor and activates a G-protein (Gs), (2) G-protein activates adenylyl cyclase, (3) adenylyl cyclase converts ATP to cAMP, (4) cAMP activates protein kinase A, (5) PKA phosphorylates a K+ channel, which closes. S53. N 8.3. T pp. 139, 172.
- [x] Consequence: lower K+ conductance raises membrane resistance and the length constant; distant or weak EPSPs reach the spike-initiation zone; NE alone changes Vm little; modulation; effect outlasts NE. N 8.3. T pp. 139-140.
- [x] Contrast: NE on alpha-2 receptors activates Gi, which suppresses adenylyl cyclase; beta receptors on heart cells enhance Ca2+ channels. N 8.3. T pp. 173-174. One line.
